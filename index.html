Working with difficult situations in daily life in a pragmatic and scientifically-based way, based on Buddha Dharma, Cognitive Behavioral theory and Neurobiology

Context & Philosophy:
I have been building this knowledge base for my own self-development and am currently reflecting on its usefulness in my daily life. This framework is heavily inspired by scientifically proven evidence and the teachings of Robert Sapolsky, Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Oakley, Anne-Laure Le Cunff, Lama Ole Nydahl, Karma Trinley Rinpoche, and Shamar Rinpoche.

⚠️ How to Use This Template (Crucial)

  • Do not read this every day. Treating a massive list as a daily checklist breeds decision fatigue, cognitive overload, and anxiety.
  • Use it as a Master Manual: This is a psychological first-aid kit. You don't apply a bandage every morning; you apply it when you are bleeding. Open specific sections only when a difficult situation approaches.
  • Customize It: Modify this template to align with your personal struggles, core values, mindset, and goals.

The "On-Demand" Decision Flow

1. Anxiety & Rumination Protocol

  • Uncontrollable Ruminations: Apply "Worry Postponement." Tell yourself, “I will think about this later at a scheduled time,” or immediately switch focus to a physical action.
  • Intrusive Thoughts: Use a physical pattern interrupt (e.g., snap your fingers) to break the cognitive loop.
  • The 0-100 Belief Check: When you notice an anxious thought, rate your actual belief in it from 0-100. Then, apply a new label and immediately park it in your Master Inbox to process later.
  • Physical Grounding: When anxious, focus entirely on the physical sensation of your feet on the floor to regulate your nervous system.
  • The Mindfulness Experiment: When the thought arises, "What if this isn't important because it's not written down?" just notice it as a thought. Don't argue. Ask yourself: "Without any notes, is this alive for me right in this moment?" The mind gradually stops demanding a system as proof of life.

2. Anger & Agitation Protocol

  • Early Detection: Notice bodily sensations (e.g., clenched jaw, fast breathing, tension) that signal agitation. Use these as early warnings to pause.
  • The Pause: Postpone your reaction. Never respond while in an afflicted state of mind.
  • Somatic Reset: Use physical interventions to calm the vagus nerve—splash cold water on your face, do an instant breathing exercise, or practice progressive muscle relaxation.

3. Information Overload & Overwhelm

When overwhelmed by tasks, tools, or open loops, mentally change their labels from "obligations" to "archives/tools."

Email / Subscriptions / Social Media
Old: “There might be something important, I need to check.”
New: “This is a content filter. I choose when to read. I won’t lose anything.”
Notes / Ideas / Wishlists
Old: “This is an open inbox, my brain has to keep it in the background.”
New: “This is an archive of ideas. It doesn’t require attention right now. I decide when to open it.”

4. Boundaries & Communication

  • The Matador Metaphor: When facing verbal abuse or intense negativity, be a Matador or grass in the wind—not a brick wall. Step aside, make an angle, and let the negative flow pass right by you without absorbing the impact.
  • Assertive Neutrality: If triggered by rejection or disagreement, respond with: “I hear you. For now, my opinion is closer to me. Everyone has their experience and their way. I have mine, and I don’t argue with yours.”
  • Instant Requests: When someone invades your boundaries or time, simply state: "I am focused on a different thing right now. I will process this and get back to you later."
  • The "Should" Trap: When someone says "You should do X," remind yourself: “I act this way because of my previous experience, biology, DNA, and environment. I don't need to defend my existence.”

5. Productivity & Cognitive Load

  • Chronos vs. Kairos Time: Shift from a rigid, goal-obsessed timesheet (Chronos) that breeds anxiety, to a dynamic model (Kairos) that focuses on the right moments and highest-impact activities. Strictly linear planning often fails in an unpredictable life.
  • Trade-offs: If you want to achieve something, you must sacrifice something else. Meaning comes from choosing which sacrifices are worth the discomfort.
  • Close Open Loops (Zeigarnik Effect): Unclosed tasks drain energy. Either do it, postpone it with a specific date, or dump it completely.
  • Head-Heart-Hand Check: If you are procrastinating or stuck, diagnose the exact failure point to fix the gap:
    • Head (Clarity): Do I know exactly what the next step is, or is the task too vague?
    • Heart (Emotion): Am I avoiding this because of fear, perfectionism, or lack of interest?
    • Hand (Resources): Do I actually have the time, tools, and physical energy to do this right now?

6. Core Mental Models & Dharma

  • Identity Shifts: Build self-control through deep "Whys." Adopt the identity of the person you want to be: "I am a person who does not react immediately, who applies Dharma in daily life, and who acts authentically rather than seeking approval."
  • Energy Protection: Never give energy to things you do not like. If you encounter a lot of negative impressions, actively collect a positive one instead (gratitude, a warm smile, friendly support).
  • Dichotomy of Control (Stoicism): Ask, “Can I control this?” If no, let it go. You cannot control others' opinions; you only control your reactions.
  • Determinism & Compassion: Behavior is shaped by biology and environment across time scales. Because we cannot change our biology or past, choose compassion over blame—for others and yourself.
  • Mindful Imprints: What we produce and throw into the world returns to us. Today's imprints in your mind are your future world. When facing a choice, ask: “What feels good and beneficial for most beings into the furthest future?”
